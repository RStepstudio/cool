How long does it take to crack a password?

The honest answer is a range, not a number, because it depends on the hardware, the algorithm, the wordlist and the target. Here is how to estimate it yourself.

Quick answer: Against a 1990 machine guessing at roughly a thousand per second, almost any modern password is unbreakable. Against today's GPU clusters guessing tens of billions per second, an 8-character password falls in seconds. That 10-billion-fold jump is the whole story.

How fast could a 1990 computer guess passwords?

A 1990 workstation with a 33 MHz or 100 MHz processor, checking a fast hash, managed on the order of a thousand to a few thousand guesses per second. Against that, a 12-character password was effectively unreachable. Password advice in 1990 focused on length because attackers were working with dial-up-era speed.

How fast can modern hardware crack passwords?

A single modern GPU rig sustains billions of hash attempts per second, and rented cloud clusters scale into the tens of billions. Because of the parallel architecture, the same algorithm runs a billion times faster than it did in 1990 with no change in method. That is why 8 characters is now hopeless and 16 characters is not.

How do I estimate the time to crack my own password?

  1. Count the character-set size you actually use: 26 lowercase, 26 uppercase, 10 digits, about 32 symbols.
  2. Raise it to the power of your password length to get the keyspace.
  3. Halve it, because on average the right answer sits halfway through the list.
  4. Divide by the guess rate: about 1,000 per second for 1990, about 10 billion per second today.

Does the storage algorithm change the answer?

Enormously. Fast legacy hashes like MD5 or SHA-1 allow billions of guesses per second, while deliberate slow hashes like bcrypt or Argon2 cut that by orders of magnitude. Any estimate should assume the weakest plausible algorithm, which is why the modern figure quoted is a worst case.

Is a long password or a complex password harder to crack?

Length. Doubling length doubles the exponent, so 12 characters is roughly 90 times harder than 6. Adding a symbol to a 6-character password is only about 30 times harder, and most of that gain is cancelled if the symbol is predictable.
