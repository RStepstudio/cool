What are the habits that actually keep a password secure?

Most password advice is either too vague or too anxious. These ten habits are ranked by how much risk each one removes.

Quick answer: Use a password manager, make every password unique and at least 16 characters, turn on two-factor authentication for anything important, and never reuse a password after a breach. Those four habits remove the overwhelming majority of real-world risk.

In brief

  • What is the single best way to secure my passwords? — Use a password manager to give every account a unique 16+ character password, then protect the vault with two-factor authentication.
  • How do I create passwords I will actually remember? — Use a passphrase of four or five unrelated words, or let a manager generate and store a random password so you never need to recall it.

Should I use a password manager?

Yes. A manager is the only realistic way to hold 60+ unique, 16-character passwords. The security of the vault rests on one strong master key plus two-factor authentication, which is a far smaller attack surface than reusing one memorable password everywhere.

Which type of two-factor authentication should I choose?

An authenticator app (TOTP) or a hardware security key. SMS codes are better than nothing but vulnerable to SIM swapping. Passkeys are even stronger where a service supports them because they are phishing-resistant by design.

How do I stop falling for phishing emails?

Hover before you click, check the real domain rather than the display text, prefer typing the address yourself, and treat any unexpected attachment as hostile. A password manager is also a good filter: it autofills only on genuine sites, so a missing autofill is a warning sign.

Is it safe to reuse one password for low-risk sites?

It is not worth the risk. Credential stuffing takes leaked pairs and replays them across thousands of sites in minutes. The sites you consider low-risk often share an email with a high-risk one, which is how chains happen.

What should I do the moment I think my account was hacked?

  1. Change the password from a trusted device and revoke other sessions.
  2. Enable two-factor authentication if it was not already on.
  3. Check recovery email, phone number, and forwarding rules for tampering.
  4. Warn contacts if the account can send messages on your behalf.
