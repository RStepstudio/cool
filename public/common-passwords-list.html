What are the most common passwords to avoid?

Attackers never start randomly. They start with the top password lists, which is why the most common passwords fall in seconds and everything else takes vastly longer.

Quick answer: Never use a password built from your name, a pet, a team, a keyboard pattern, or a common word plus digits. Every one of those is in a wordlist a cracking rig can try in microseconds.

In brief

  • What are the most common passwords right now? — Sequential and keyboard patterns, names, popular words and brand names, usually with a year or a single symbol appended. They are all in eve…
  • Is P@ssw0rd a good password? — No. It is one of the first variants a cracking tool generates, so it adds almost no real protection over the word password.
  • How do I remember a strong password? — Use a passphrase of four or five unrelated words, or let a password manager generate and store a random password for you.
  • Does adding a number at the end really help? — Barely. Cracking tools try every common suffix automatically, so a single predictable digit adds almost no entropy.
  • How do I know if my password is on a common list? — Check it with a strength tool that also flags dictionary words and patterns, and confirm the password is not already known to be breached.

Which passwords are tried first by crackers?

  • Sequences: 123456, qwerty, abcdef, 1q2w3e4r
  • Names and brands: michael, jennifer, iloveyou, admin
  • Seasonal and business patterns: summer2024, company123, welcome1
  • Keyboard walks: qwertyuiop, asdfghjkl, 1qaz2wsx
  • Password puns: p@ssw0rd, passw0rd, letmein

Why do capitalisation tricks and added symbols fail?

Because the rules are standard. Every cracking tool knows to try the word lowercase, then Capitalised, then with a digit appended, then with "!". "P@ssw0rd" is one of the first hundred variants a tool generates, so it adds almost no real entropy over "password".

What should I do instead of a memorable password?

Use a passphrase of four or five unrelated words with no relationship to you. Check it with the PassMeter checker: if it reports fewer than 60 bits, keep going until it is comfortably above 80.

Do common passwords still work in 2026?

Better than ever. Cracking hardware has scaled faster than most people change habits, and credential stuffing replays leaked lists against new services automatically. A "unique enough" password that is still a dictionary word remains one of the most common causes of takeover.

Is my favourite password on a common list?

Almost certainly, if it is short, common, or a real word with predictable edits. The only reliable way to know is to test it, and to test it in a tool that sends nothing to a server.
