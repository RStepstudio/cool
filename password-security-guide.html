What does good password security look like in 2026?

Three layers decide whether an account survives an attack: the password you choose, how the service stores it, and whether a second factor is required.

Quick answer: Strong unique passwords, sites that hash with slow algorithms like bcrypt or Argon2, and mandatory 2FA. If a service fails the second or third test, choose a different service.

How are passwords stored by websites?

They should never be stored in readable form. Modern sites hash passwords with a deliberately slow, salted algorithm such as bcrypt, scrypt or Argon2. The salt is unique per user, so two identical passwords produce different hashes, and the slowness makes offline brute force expensive. The OWASP Password Storage Cheat Sheet is the reference here.

What is a dictionary attack?

An attacker tries a list of common passwords first, in order, against every account. This is why "123456", "qwerty" and "password" fall in seconds. It costs the attacker almost nothing and catches a large share of real accounts.

What is credential stuffing?

Automating the same idea at scale: take username and password pairs from one breach and replay them against thousands of other services. Because people reuse passwords, one breach cascades. Unique passwords break the chain completely.

How does two-factor authentication reduce account takeover?

A stolen password alone is no longer enough. The attacker would also need the second factor, which is either something you have (a device, a hardware key) or something you know. Passkeys improve this further by binding the credential to the site, so a phishing page cannot collect a usable credential.

Should I reuse a password for a low-value throwaway account?

No. Low-value accounts frequently share email addresses or recovery channels with high-value ones, and their own logs can leak the credential. Let a manager handle it, or accept that uniqueness is the cheapest real protection.

What does "salted and hashed" mean for me as a user?

It means a site breach does not hand attackers your plaintext passwords, and identical passwords across sites look different in the leak. It also means bad hashing can still be cracked offline, so length still matters. Good practice on both sides is the goal.
